## Who is who

| Role | Who | What this person does |
|---|---|---|
| **Reviewer 1** | **Dr Waad** | Reads all 400 pairs and fills in this workbook, working alone. |
| **Reviewer 2** | **Dr Ainaa** | Reads the same 400 pairs and fills in this workbook, working alone — never seeing Dr Waad's answers. |
| **Reviewer 3** | **Dr Amani Salman** | Reads only the pairs where Dr Waad and Dr Ainaa gave **different** answers, and decides the final answer. Roughly 60 pairs out of the 400. |
| **Checking reviewer** | **Dr Samer Dahdoul** | Separately re-reads a **random 80** of the 400 pairs, without seeing anyone else's answers. |
| **Principal investigator** | **Dr Lami Qurt** | Runs the study and does the analysis. Deliberately makes **no judgements** on any pair. |

**Why Dr Amani is kept out of the first round.** If all three doctors read every pair together there is nothing left to measure. The whole point is to find out how often two doctors reading the same record **disagree**, and then settle those disagreements in a recorded way.

**Why Dr Samer re-reads a random sample rather than the disagreements.** When Dr Waad and Dr Ainaa *disagree*, Dr Amani settles it. But if they both make the same mistake in the same direction, they agree — and nothing flags it. A published study of exactly this task found that trainees called a return "a possible medical problem" less often than senior doctors (9% against 13%). Dr Samer's random 80 is how we find out whether that happened here. It is a **check on the method, not a correction of anyone's work**: those codes are reported separately and do not overwrite anything.

> **A note on wording.** The study protocol and the published paper call Dr Amani's role the **adjudicator**, and call the settling of a disagreement **adjudication**. Those are the formal terms and they mean exactly what is described above. This workbook says **Reviewer 3** throughout, because that is clearer to work from.

## Part A — Read this once, before your first pair

**1. Two layers, and the order between them.** Steps 2 and 4 record **facts only** — what is written in the record, and only the handful of facts the hospital export cannot supply. Steps 6 to 9 are **judgements**. Both reviewers should record identical facts; if you disagree on a fact, one of you made an abstraction error. Disagreeing on a judgement is normal and expected, and is what the study measures.

**2. Every judgement points back to a field.** Each judgement asks you to name **which field** drove it. In the spreadsheet you **pick the field from a list** — you never type the code, so it cannot be mistyped.

Two judgements — **Step 3 and Step 7** — also ask for **one short line of reasoning**. Those two carry the paper. The other three need the pointer only.

**Never copy the contents of a field into a Basis.** What you wrote in R-1 or R-3 stays in R-1 and R-3, and the pointer is how it is found again. Writing it twice creates two versions that can disagree, and then nobody can tell which one is right.

**3. Not documented is never the same as normal.** If nothing is written, tick **Not documented**. Do not tick normal because the child looked well, because the doctor is reliable, or because it was probably done and not written. This single rule decides whether the study is honest.

**4. You will not see who treated the child.** The treating doctor's name is removed from the records before they reach you. This is deliberate. You are being asked whether a discharge decision was reasonable, and that judgement must not depend on who made it — especially in a small department where you work alongside these colleagues every day. If you recognise a case anyway, do not guess and do not ask: record it in Step 10 and hand the pair to Dr Amani Salman.

**5. What counts as the record.** In scope: the nursing entry at arrival, the physician note, the nursing notes, the investigation results, the prescription, and the discharge instruction field of that same encounter. Out of scope: earlier visits, later visits, and anything you know personally about the patient or the clinician.

**6. Work alone.** Do not discuss any pair with the other reviewer until Dr Amani Salman has settled the disagreements. Do not compare files. Do not ask what the other reviewer put for a given pair.

**7. If the record contradicts itself,** record both statements and say so in Step 10. Do not silently choose one.

## Step 2 — Index visit: what only a reader can tell

**Read the whole index record.** The presenting complaint, the diagnosis, the medications, the investigations and the times are all in the hospital export already — you need them to judge, but you never retype them.

Record only these four.

| # | Field | Record |
|---|---|---|
| I-1 | Vital signs documented | ☐ All documented  ☐ Partly documented  ☐ None documented |
| I-2 | Was the body system relevant to the presenting complaint examined and documented? | ☐ Yes  ☐ No  ☐ Cannot tell |
| I-3 | Discharge instructions | ☐ None documented  ☐ Generic advice only  ☐ Specific return precautions |
| I-4 | Follow-up directed to | ☐ None documented  ☐ Return to ED  ☐ Outpatient clinic  ☐ Primary health centre  ☐ Other: |

> **I-2 is the field that most often decides Step 7.** A child with a limp whose hip was never examined is a different case from one whose hip was examined and recorded as normal. Both may end in septic arthritis; they are not the same case.

> **I-1 asks what was written, not whether it was normal.** If no respiratory rate appears anywhere in the record, that is *None* or *Partly*, however well the child looked.

> **I-2 — when is it No, and when is it Cannot tell?** This trips people up, so the rule is fixed:
> - **No** — you can read the note, and the relevant examination is not in it. A limp with no hip or knee examination recorded is **No**.
> - **Cannot tell** — you cannot work out *which* examination was the relevant one, because the presenting complaint itself is missing or unreadable.
>
> **Nothing written down is No, not Cannot tell.** *Cannot tell* describes a limit on **your** ability to judge, never the clinician's failure to record.

## Step 3 — Index visit: your judgement, locked

Answer this **before** you know what happened at the return visit. This ordering is the whole reason the steps are numbered.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| On what is documented above, was the assessment and the decision to discharge reasonable? | ☐ Adequate  ☐ Inadequate  ☐ Cannot tell from the record |
| **Basis — field** | pick from the list: I-1 … R-4, or *more than one* |
| **Basis — reason** | one short line |
| **Your initials and today's date** | |

**The field says where you looked. The reason says what you concluded.** They are two different cells and they do two different jobs. **What the record does *not* say is a fact too**, and is often the whole reason for an *Inadequate*.

| | Field | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| ✓ | `I-2` | `hip not examined anywhere in the note` |
| ✓ | `more than one` | `I-1 and I-3 — vitals complete and specific precautions given` |
| ✗ | `I-2` | `looked fine` — that is not a reason |
| ✗ | `I-2` | `chest clear, throat red` — that is the field's contents, copied. It is already recorded; do not write it again |

One line is enough. You are not writing an argument, you are naming what decided it.

**Why you sign it.** Your initials record that you reached this judgement **before** you opened the return visit. Two initials and the date is all that is needed — `W.Q. 2026-09-14`.

Be clear about what the signature is and is not: **nothing in the file physically prevents you from coming back and changing this after Step 4.** It is an agreement, not a lock. The study depends on you keeping it. If you change your mind after reading the return visit, **leave this box exactly as it is** and explain in Step 10 — that correction is data, and it is far more valuable than a tidy answer.

## Step 4 — Return visit: what only a reader can tell

Now open the return visit. Again, the diagnosis, disposition, treatment and times come from the export. Record only these four.

| # | Field | Record |
|---|---|---|
| R-1 | Reason for return — copy the exact words of the family or the nurse | ☐ None documented  ☐ Copied here: |
| R-2 | Is the return diagnosis different from the index diagnosis? | ☐ Yes  ☐ No |
| R-3 | Any statement about not being able to access care — appointment, transport, distance, checkpoint, cost, medicine not obtained | ☐ None documented  ☐ Documented, copy the exact words: |
| R-4 | Any statement about whether the medicine was actually obtained or given | ☐ None documented  ☐ Documented, copy the exact words: |

> **R-1, R-3 and R-4 must be copied exactly as written.** These three carry Step 9 and the reasons table in the paper.

> **Copy the exact words means copy the exact words.** Do not shorten them, tidy them, or turn them into a conclusion. If the note says *mother reports no clinic appointment until next month*, write that. Do not write *access problem* — that is already your judgement, and once it is written that way nobody can check it.
>
> **If the note is in Arabic, copy the Arabic.** Translating as you go is interpreting. Translation happens later, once, with the original kept beside it. Copy it even if it is badly written or misspelled: you are recording what the record says, not what it should have said.


> **R-2 is a clinical judgement, not a comparison of two labels.** "Fever" then "pneumonia" is the same illness better characterised, so the answer is No.

## Step 6 — Judgement 1: Relatedness

Is the return about the same problem as the index visit?

> **Step 6 and Step 7 are not the same question, and reviewers regularly confuse them.** Step 6 asks whether this is the **same problem**. Step 7 asks **why the child came back**. One does not answer the other.

Take one child and change only the ending:

| 2-year-old seen for fever and discharged. Then... | Step 6 | Step 7 |
|---|---|---|
| Returns 36 h later with pneumonia that was not detectable at the index visit | Related | Illness-related |
| Returns 36 h later, child well on examination, mother wanted reassurance | Related | Patient-related |
| Returns 36 h later with pneumonia, and the chest was never examined at the index visit | Related | Physician-related |
| Returns 36 h later with a fractured arm after falling | Unrelated | Does not apply |

The first three rows are all the same illness, so all three are **related**. What differs is the reason the child is standing in the ED again — and that is Step 7, not Step 6. Relatedness on its own never tells you whether anything went wrong.

**Step 6 is a gate.** If you code **unrelated**, leave Step 7 blank. There is nothing to attribute, because the return had nothing to do with the index visit.

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ☐ 1 Related | Same illness, a complication of it, a progression of it, or a side effect of treatment given for it |
| ☐ 2 Unrelated | A genuinely different problem |
| ☐ 3 Uncertain | The record does not allow a decision |

**Basis — field only.** Pick the field that showed you this is the same problem, or a different one. Usually `R-1` or `R-2`.

**No written reason is needed here.** Relatedness is rarely contested, and the field on its own is enough for Dr Amani to check your answer against the record.

Field: ☐ I-1 ☐ I-2 ☐ I-3 ☐ I-4 ☐ R-1 ☐ R-2 ☐ R-3 ☐ R-4 ☐ more than one ☐ none fit

Judge the clinical problem, not the wording of the label. "Fever" at index and "pneumonia" at return is **related** — the same illness, better characterised the second time. A new injury in a child previously seen for illness is **unrelated**. In genuine doubt, choose **related**; that keeps the case in the safety-relevant group rather than discarding it.

## Step 7 — Judgement 2: Attribution

**Answer this only if you coded Step 6 as related or uncertain.** If the return was unrelated, leave this step blank.

What best explains why this child came back? Choose **one** — the factor that contributed most.

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ☐ 1 Illness-related | The disease took its natural course. Care at the index visit was appropriate for what was knowable at the time |
| ☐ 2 Patient-related | Driven mainly by caregiver factors — anxiety, instructions not followed, medicine not given, seeking reassurance with no clinical change |
| ☐ 3 Physician-related | Something about the index encounter could reasonably have been done differently with the information available at the time |
| ☐ 4 Uncertain | The categories cannot be separated |

**Basis — field, then one short line of reasoning.** This is the judgement the paper turns on, so it keeps a written reason.

| | Field | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| illness-related | `I-2` | `chest examined and recorded clear; pneumonia not detectable then` |
| physician-related | `I-2` | `chest never examined` |

Field: ☐ I-1 ☐ I-2 ☐ I-3 ☐ I-4 ☐ R-1 ☐ R-2 ☐ R-3 ☐ R-4 ☐ more than one ☐ none fit

Reason: ______________________________________________

**The rule that keeps this fair:** judge only on what was documented and knowable at the index visit. Never judge with hindsight. An illness that could not reasonably have been diagnosed then is **illness-related**, even if the outcome was serious. The question is never whether something bad happened — it is whether the decision was reasonable given what was in front of the clinician.

If two categories both apply, choose the one that contributed most. If you truly cannot separate them, code 4. Do not guess in order to avoid an uncertain code.

## Step 8 — Judgement 3: Preventability

Could different action at the index visit plausibly have prevented this return?

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ☐ 1 Preventable | Different assessment, treatment, or instructions would probably have avoided it |
| ☐ 2 Not preventable | The return was an appropriate response to a genuine change |
| ☐ 3 Uncertain | Cannot determine |

**Basis — field only.** Which field, if any, could have changed the outcome. Often `I-3`.

**No written reason is needed here** — the reasoning overlaps almost entirely with Step 7, which already carries one.

Field: ☐ I-1 ☐ I-2 ☐ I-3 ☐ I-4 ☐ R-1 ☐ R-2 ☐ R-3 ☐ R-4 ☐ more than one ☐ none fit

Preventable is **not** the same as physician-related. A return driven by a misunderstanding may well have been preventable by clearer instructions, and is still patient-related. Keep the two judgements separate.

## Step 9 — Judgement 4: Access barrier

Did the family come back to the ED because the alternative they were directed to was closed to them?

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ☐ 1 Yes | A barrier is **written down** in R-3 or R-4 |
| ☐ 2 No | No such barrier is written down. **This includes a record that says nothing at all about access — silence is No, not Uncertain** |
| ☐ 3 Uncertain | The record gestures at a barrier without stating one — for example *"family could not manage the follow-up"* with no reason given |

**If 1, which barrier:** ☐ No clinic appointment available  ☐ Could not reach the clinic — transport, distance, checkpoint  ☐ Could not obtain the medicine  ☐ Cost  ☐ Other:

**Basis — field only:** ☐ R-3 ☐ R-4 ☐ both ☐ none — nothing documented

**Do not retype the words.** They are already recorded in R-3 and R-4, and the pointer is how they are found. If you coded **2 no**, choose *none — nothing documented*.

Code **1 only on documented evidence.** If the record is silent, code 2, not 3. This judgement is **independent of Step 7**: a case can be illness-related and still carry an access barrier. Record both.

## Part B — Working rules

1. **No more than 25 pairs at a sitting.** Agreement falls as concentration falls.
2. **Do not revisit earlier pairs** once you have moved on, unless the PI asks you to. Drift corrected quietly is invisible; drift left in place is measurable.
3. **Do not look up what happened to the child after the return visit.** Judge the pair only.
4. **Uncertain is a real answer.** A reported uncertainty rate is a normal, publishable finding. A guess is a hidden error.
5. **Nothing leaves institutional equipment.** No patient identifiers in any file, message, or photograph.

---

## Part C — The pilot

The first **40 pairs** are a pilot. Both reviewers classify all 40, and then, as a group:

- kappa is calculated,
- every disagreement is opened and discussed,
- this workbook is corrected where the rules proved unclear,
- and it is reissued as **version 1.0**.

The pilot classifications are then **discarded**, and those 40 pairs are done again under version 1.0 with the rest. Only version 1.0 results enter the analysis. The pilot is the only point at which the two reviewers discuss cases.

### What to notice

**1. A serious outcome, and the judgement is still that care was reasonable.** The child was admitted with pneumonia, and Step 7 is still *illness-related*. Step 7 asks whether the decision was sound **with what was in front of the clinician**, never whether the ending was bad. If a bad outcome by itself made a case physician-related, the study would measure nothing but bad luck.

**2. Every judgement names a field, and nothing is written twice.** Five judgements, five pointers, but only **two** written reasons — Step 3 and Step 7. Anyone can take this form back to the record and check every judgement, and no fact appears in two places where the two copies could disagree.

**3. Absence is a fact, and it gets a pointer too.** Step 8 points at `I-1` precisely *because* the respiratory rate is missing. Step 9 records *none — nothing documented*. You are recording what the record does not say as carefully as what it does.

**4. Uncertain was used, not dodged.** Step 8 is genuinely undecidable here, so it is coded 3. A reported uncertainty rate is a normal finding. A guess made to avoid coding 3 is a hidden error that nobody can ever detect.

**5. The Arabic was copied as Arabic.** Not translated, not summarised, not turned into *"mother reports breathlessness"*.

**6. The two reviewers disagreed here — and both were right to.** On this same record Dr Ainaa answered Step 3 **Inadequate**, with the Basis `I-1 — no respiratory rate recorded in a febrile child`. That is a defensible reading. So is Dr Waad's *Adequate*. Both named a field, both pointed at a real fact, neither guessed.

**This is exactly the disagreement the study exists to measure.** It goes to Dr Amani, who decides the final answer. **Neither reviewer did anything wrong, and neither should change their answer to match the other.** If you find yourself softening a judgement because you imagine the other reviewer disagreeing, the study has lost the thing it was built to find.
