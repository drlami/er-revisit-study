# Sample Reviewer Data — SYNTHETIC

**Study:** Unscheduled 72-hour return visits to the pediatric ED, PMC Ramallah
**Purpose:** worked example of how 40 pilot pairs are recorded and analysed
**Created:** 2026-08-18

---

## THESE ARE NOT REAL PATIENTS

Every case below is **invented**. No record was consulted, no patient exists, and no number here describes anything that happened at PMC or anywhere else. The cases were written to look plausible so that the arithmetic and the layout can be tested before the real data arrives.

**Do not cite any figure in this document. Do not put it in the ethics file. Do not circulate it without this page attached.** When the real pilot is done, this file is deleted.

---

## 1. What this document is for

Three things:

- to show the reviewers what a completed set of 40 pairs looks like;
- to show what has to be typed into the spreadsheet, and in what shape;
- to show what comes out at the end, and how it is read.

The workbook records far more per pair than appears here. This document keeps only the codes, because the codes are what the reliability analysis uses.

---

## 2. Code key

**R1 is Reviewer 1 and R2 is Reviewer 2** — the two physicians who classify every pair independently, without seeing each other's work. Where their codes match, they agreed. Where the codes differ, the pair goes to **Reviewer 3** — the senior pediatrician who decides the final answer — and it is those differences that kappa measures.

| Judgement | Codes |
|---|---|
| Eligibility (Step 5) | 1 eligible, 2 scheduled, 3 administrative, 4 insufficient documentation |
| Relatedness (Step 6) | 1 related, 2 unrelated, 3 uncertain |
| Attribution (Step 7) | 1 illness-related, 2 patient-related, 3 physician-related, 4 uncertain |
| Preventability (Step 8) | 1 preventable, 2 not preventable, 3 uncertain |
| Access barrier (Step 9) | 1 yes, 2 no, 3 uncertain |

A dash means the field does not apply: the pair was excluded at Step 5, or the return was coded unrelated, in which case attribution is not recorded.

| Group | n | Effect |
|---|---|---|

| **Eligibility disputed** between reviewers (pair 36) | 1 | Removed from the four kappas; Reviewer 3 decides eligibility |

> **The denominators are not all the same, and that is correct.** Each judgement is computed on the pairs where both reviewers actually made that judgement. Report the denominator next to every kappa, or a reader cannot check your arithmetic.

## 8. How to read this

**Compare the first two rows, and the whole problem with kappa is in front of you.**

| | Reviewers agreed on | Kappa |
|---|---|---|
| Relatedness | 34 of 36, 94.4% | 0.64 |
| Attribution | 29 of 34, 85.3% | 0.76 |

The reviewers agreed **more often** on relatedness than on attribution, and kappa is **lower** for it. That is not an error. Nearly every return in this sample was coded *related*, so two reviewers guessing blindly would still have agreed most of the time, and kappa subtracts that expected agreement. Attribution was spread across several categories, so agreement there was harder to reach by chance and kappa rewards it.

This is why the agreement percentage and PABAK sit beside every kappa. A reader given the kappa alone would rank your relatedness judgement as the weaker of the two, when the reviewers actually disagreed about it half as often.

Confidence intervals are truncated at 1.00, because kappa cannot exceed it. Every kappa here carries a wide interval, because 40 pairs is a small number. **The pilot cannot certify reliability. It can only detect disaster.** The kappa you publish comes from the full 400.

> **This fictional sample passes on every criterion. Do not expect that.** It was written by one person in one sitting, so it is more consistent than two tired reviewers working separately over three weeks will be. A real pilot that comes back amber on attribution or preventability is the normal result, not a failure.

---

## 9. What to do with the disagreements

Sort each one into exactly one of three bins:

1. **Abstraction error** — one reviewer missed something that was written in the chart. Fix by retraining, not by changing the rules.
2. **Rule ambiguity** — the workbook genuinely did not say. This, and only this, changes the workbook.
3. **Genuine clinical disagreement** — same facts, both defensible. Leave it alone.

In this fictional sample, pair 6 (asthma discharged without a steroid) and pair 28 (gastroenteritis with no hydration status documented) are the third kind. Writing a rule to force those into line would manufacture agreement, and the kappa you then report would mean nothing.

Pair 36 is the second kind: one reviewer read *attend the clinic in 3 days* as a scheduled return, the other did not. That is a wording gap in Step 5, and it is exactly what the pilot exists to find.

## 10. What you actually have to type

Section 4 is the whole input. Nine columns, forty rows, in Excel. Nothing else is needed to produce section 7.

Enter it exactly as shown, with a dash for fields that do not apply. Send that sheet and the analysis comes back. No statistical software has to be installed on your machine, and no one has to learn SPSS to get this table.
